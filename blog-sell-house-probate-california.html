How to Sell a House in Probate in California: Authority, Timelines, and Taxes

By Patrick Mahoney October 26, 2026 11 min read

Someone close to you has died and left a house. Maybe you are the executor named in the will, or there was no will and the family is looking at you. Either way, you have a court process you did not ask for, relatives with opinions, and a house that costs money every month while you work out what you are allowed to do.

This guide covers how selling a house in probate works in California: when probate is required, who has authority to sign, court-confirmed sales versus full authority, how long it takes, and what Prop 19 and the stepped-up basis mean for the taxes. If the house came to you outside of probate, our guide to selling an inherited house in California covers that side. Probate is one of the situations we help with most often.

What probate means for selling the house

Probate is the court process that moves a deceased person's property to the people entitled to it. In California it is generally required when someone dies owning real estate in their own name. A will says who gets what; it does not skip the court. The main exceptions are a house held in a living trust, which the trustee can sell without the court; a house held in joint tenancy or as community property with right of survivorship, where the survivor records an affidavit of death; and a house with a recorded transfer-on-death deed. California also has simplified small-estate procedures, including one for a modest primary residence, but the value limits change, so check with the court's self-help center or an attorney before assuming you qualify.

Who has the authority to sell

Until the court appoints someone, nobody can sign a deed. The person appointed is the personal representative. An executor named in a will receives Letters Testamentary; with no will, a family member petitions to be administrator and receives Letters of Administration. Title companies need those Letters before insuring a sale. A trustee of a living trust is different: the trust itself gives the power to sell, and title wants a certification of trust plus the death certificate.

Heirs do not sign the sale. The personal representative signs for the estate, and the proceeds go into an estate account. One sibling cannot block a sale by refusing to sign, because that signature was never required. An heir who objects takes it to the court, which brings us to the two kinds of probate sale.

Full authority versus court confirmation

Most personal representatives ask for full authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act, the IAEA. With full authority, the representative sells much like any owner, with one extra step: a Notice of Proposed Action mailed to the heirs describing the sale terms. If no one objects within the notice period, generally fifteen days, the sale proceeds without a hearing. If someone objects, or the court granted only limited authority, the court must confirm the sale. A probate referee appraises the property, the accepted price generally must be at least ninety percent of that appraisal, and anyone can overbid at the hearing. The first overbid must beat the accepted price by ten percent of the first ten thousand dollars plus five percent of the rest. A buyer can lose the house at that hearing, which is why many avoid confirmation sales and the rest price in the risk.

How long it takes

Filing to first hearing commonly runs several weeks to a couple of months, depending on the county. After Letters issue, creditors get a four-month window to make claims, and the estate cannot close before that runs. In practice, most probates take somewhere between nine months and a year and a half, with contested estates and court-confirmed sales at the long end. You do not have to wait for probate to close. The sale usually happens in the middle, once Letters issue, and the proceeds sit in the estate account until the court approves the final accounting.

Prop 19 and the stepped-up basis

Two tax rules shape the timing. First, Proposition 19. Since early 2021, a child who inherits a parent's house keeps the parent's low property tax base only by moving in as a primary residence within a year and filing for the homeowners' exemption. If nobody moves in, the county reassesses the house to market value as of the date of death, and the bill can multiply. The sale does not trigger the reassessment; it already happened, and every month the estate holds the house it accrues that higher tax. Selling sooner stops the meter. Second, the stepped-up basis. For income tax, the house's cost basis generally resets to its fair market value on the date of death. Sell for roughly that value and there is little or no capital gain to tax. Hold it and sell for more later, and the gain above the date-of-death value is taxable. Confirm your own numbers with a CPA.

Your options, honestly

  • List with an agent. If the house is in good shape, the estate has full authority, the heirs agree, and nobody needs the money quickly, listing will usually bring the highest price. Pick an agent who has closed probate sales. Expect repair requests, financing that falls through, and a longer timeline than promised.
  • Sell to a cash buyer. If the house is dated, full of belongings, needs court confirmation, or the heirs are spread across the country, a cash sale trades some price for certainty and speed. No repairs, no cleaning, no lender, and a closing date that lines up with your Letters. Our comparison of cash buyers and agents in California goes deeper on the trade-off.
  • Keep it or rent it. If an heir can move in within the Prop 19 window, keeping it may preserve the tax base. Renting it makes the personal representative a landlord during probate, with a reassessed tax bill and duties to the other heirs.
  • Wait until probate closes and sell as co-owners. Usually the worst path. Once the house is distributed, every heir is on title and must sign, and one holdout can force a partition lawsuit. Selling inside the estate avoids that.

What to have ready before you call

  • The death certificate, or at least the date of death.
  • Whether there is a will, a trust, or neither, and who is named as executor or trustee.
  • Where the probate stands: not yet filed, filed with a hearing date, or Letters issued, and whether the Letters grant full or limited authority.
  • Your probate attorney's name, if you have one.
  • Any mortgage, reverse mortgage, or lien you know about, and the latest property tax bill.
  • A rough list of who the heirs are and where they live.
  • The condition of the house in plain terms: roof, foundation, unpermitted work, how full it is.

How this works across Northern California

Probate is state law, so the rules above apply everywhere in California. What changes by county is the court itself: where it sits, how far out the probate calendar runs, and how the clerk and examiners handle paperwork. Those details drive your timing.

When you need a professional first

Talk to a probate attorney before you sign anything if there is no will, the will is contested, an heir is a minor or cannot be located, the house has a reverse mortgage with a lender deadline, or you are unsure whether a small-estate procedure applies. Many attorneys take a statutory fee paid from the estate at the end, so you may not need cash up front. Talk to a CPA about the stepped-up basis and the estate's tax return. Call the county assessor about Prop 19 if any heir may move in, because the one-year deadline has real consequences. None of this is legal advice, and a short call with a professional at the start saves months at the end.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my parents' house before probate is finished in California?

Yes. Most probate sales happen in the middle of the process, not after it. Once the court issues Letters to the personal representative, that person can sign a purchase contract and close escrow. The proceeds go into the estate's bank account and are distributed to heirs when the court approves the final accounting. What you cannot do is sign a deed before Letters are issued, so the date of your first hearing drives everything.

Do all the heirs have to agree to sell a house in probate?

Generally no. The personal representative signs for the estate; individual heirs do not sign the deed. Under full IAEA authority, heirs receive a Notice of Proposed Action and have a short window, usually fifteen days, to object. If someone objects, the sale goes to the court for confirmation rather than being blocked outright. If the estate has only limited authority, every sale needs court confirmation anyway.

What happens to the property taxes on an inherited house in California?

Under Proposition 19, the house keeps the parent's low assessed value only if a child moves in as a primary residence within a year and claims the homeowners' exemption. Otherwise the county reassesses it to market value as of the date of death, and the estate receives supplemental tax bills for the difference. The reassessment happens whether or not you sell, which is one reason estates tend to sell sooner.

Will I owe capital gains tax if I sell an inherited house?

Often very little. Inherited property generally receives a stepped-up basis, meaning its cost basis for tax purposes resets to the fair market value on the date of death. If the estate sells close to that value, the gain is small or zero. If you hold the house and it appreciates, the gain above the date-of-death value is taxable. Confirm the numbers with a CPA, since the estate's situation affects the answer.

Can you buy a probate house that still has everything in it?

Yes. We buy estate houses exactly as they sit, with the furniture, boxes, paperwork, and whatever else is left behind. Take what the family wants and leave the rest. We also work with executors and heirs who live out of state, using a mobile notary and a local title company so nobody has to travel to close.
