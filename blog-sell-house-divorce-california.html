How to Sell a House During Divorce in California

By Patrick Mahoney June 13, 2026 7 min read

Divorce is one of the most stressful events a person can go through. When you add the question of what to do with the family home, the emotional weight and financial complexity multiply. The marital home is usually the single largest asset a couple owns, and deciding how to handle it can become one of the most contentious parts of the entire divorce process.

If you and your spouse are going through a divorce in California and need to figure out what to do with the house, this guide will walk you through your legal obligations under community property law, your three main options for the home, and the practical realities of selling a property when two parties who may not see eye to eye need to agree on every detail.

California Community Property Law and Your Home

California is one of nine community property states in the U.S. Under California Family Code, any asset acquired during the marriage is generally considered community property and is subject to an equal 50/50 division between both spouses upon divorce. This includes the family home if it was purchased during the marriage, regardless of whose name is on the title or who made the mortgage payments.

There are a few important nuances to understand:

  • Community property presumption: If the home was purchased after the date of marriage and before the date of separation, it is presumed to be community property. Both spouses have an equal ownership interest.
  • Separate property contributions: If one spouse used separate property funds (such as an inheritance or money owned before the marriage) for the down payment, that spouse may be entitled to reimbursement of that contribution before the remaining equity is split.
  • Commingled funds: When separate and community funds are mixed together over time (e.g., one spouse's inheritance was deposited into a joint account used for mortgage payments), tracing the separate property contribution can become complicated and may require forensic accounting.
  • Both spouses must agree or a court decides: Neither spouse can unilaterally sell the home. Both must agree to the sale, or the court must order it. During divorce proceedings, California courts often issue automatic temporary restraining orders (ATROs) that prevent either spouse from selling, transferring, or encumbering major assets without the other's written consent or a court order.

Understanding these rules is the starting point for making an informed decision about your home. With that foundation, let's look at the three paths forward.

Option 1: One Spouse Buys Out the Other

In some divorces, one spouse wants to keep the home — often because children are involved and the family wants to minimize disruption, or because one spouse has a strong emotional attachment to the property. In a buyout, the spouse keeping the home compensates the other for their share of the equity.

Here is how the process typically works:

  1. Get an appraisal: Both parties agree on a professional appraiser (or each hires one) to determine the home's current fair market value.
  2. Calculate equity: Subtract the remaining mortgage balance and any agreed-upon selling costs from the appraised value. The result is the total equity. Each spouse is generally entitled to half.
  3. Refinance the mortgage: The spouse keeping the home must refinance the mortgage into their name alone. This removes the departing spouse from all financial liability. The refinance must be approved based on the keeping spouse's income and credit alone, which can be a significant hurdle — especially if the household previously relied on two incomes.
  4. Execute a quitclaim deed: The departing spouse signs a quitclaim deed transferring their ownership interest to the keeping spouse.

When this works well: The keeping spouse has strong enough income and credit to qualify for a refinance, both parties agree on the home's value, and there is enough liquid cash or equity to fund the buyout payment.

When this falls apart: The keeping spouse cannot qualify for a refinance on a single income. The parties cannot agree on an appraised value. The buyout amount is so large that neither party has the cash, and the keeping spouse cannot pull enough equity from a cash-out refinance.

Option 2: Sell on the Open Market

Selling the home on the open market through a real estate agent is the most common approach when neither spouse wants to (or can afford to) keep the home. The property is listed, shown to buyers, and sold to the highest bidder. The proceeds after paying off the mortgage, agent commissions, and closing costs are then divided between the spouses.

While this can maximize the sale price, the process introduces significant practical challenges for divorcing couples:

  • Agreement on listing price: Both spouses must agree on the asking price. One may want to price high and wait for maximum return; the other may want to price low and close quickly. This disagreement alone can delay the process by weeks or months.
  • Agent selection: Both parties must agree on which real estate agent to hire. If trust has broken down between the spouses, agreeing on a third party to represent their shared interests can be difficult.
  • Repair and staging decisions: Should you spend $15,000 on repairs and staging to get a higher price, or sell as-is? Who pays for the improvements? Who manages the contractors? These questions require cooperation that may not exist.
  • Showing schedules: If one spouse is still living in the home, coordinating showings requires ongoing communication and flexibility — two things that are often in short supply during a contentious divorce.
  • Timeline: A traditional sale in California takes 3-6 months on average, and sometimes longer. When you add the time needed for both parties to agree on each step, the process can stretch even further. Meanwhile, the divorce proceedings may be on hold waiting for the home sale to close.
  • Both signatures required: Every document — the listing agreement, purchase contract, counteroffers, disclosures, closing documents — requires both spouses' signatures. If one spouse becomes uncooperative or unresponsive, the entire transaction can stall.

For couples who are on reasonably good terms and have the patience to work through a traditional sale, this option can yield the highest net proceeds. But for many divorcing couples, the friction and timeline make it impractical.

Option 3: Sell to a Cash Home Buyer

For couples who want the fastest, cleanest path to dividing the home's value and moving on with their lives, selling to a cash home buyer offers significant advantages during divorce.

Here is why many divorcing couples in California choose this route:

  • Speed: A cash sale can close in as few as 7-14 days. There is no waiting for buyer financing approval, no appraisal contingencies, and no drawn-out negotiation. Both parties receive their share of the proceeds quickly, allowing the divorce to proceed without a lingering real estate transaction holding everything up.
  • Sell as-is: No repairs, no staging, no cleaning, no showings. The home is purchased in its current condition. This eliminates the need for both spouses to agree on renovation decisions, hire contractors, or coordinate access for repairs.
  • Minimal coordination: A cash sale requires far fewer decisions between the parties. There is no listing price debate, no agent to select together, no showing schedule to manage, and no back-and-forth with retail buyers. Both spouses simply agree to accept the offer and sign the closing documents.
  • Certainty: Cash offers do not fall through due to financing issues, which happen in roughly 15-20% of traditional sales. Once you accept the offer, you can count on closing.
  • Emotional relief: Every day the home sits unsold is another day both parties remain financially and emotionally tied together. A fast cash sale provides a clean break, which is often what both spouses need most.

The trade-off is that a cash offer will typically be below full market value — often 70-85% of what you might receive on the open market. But when you factor in agent commissions (5-6%), repair costs, carrying costs during a long listing period, and the emotional toll of a drawn-out process, the net difference is often smaller than people expect.

Practical Challenges of Selling During Divorce

Regardless of which selling method you choose, there are practical hurdles that arise when two people who are ending their relationship must cooperate on a major financial transaction. Understanding these challenges in advance can help you plan accordingly.

Communication Breakdown

Effective real estate transactions require clear, timely communication between all parties. During a divorce, direct communication between spouses may be limited, hostile, or routed through attorneys — adding delays and cost at every step. Cash sales minimize the number of decisions and communications required, which is one reason divorcing couples often prefer them.

Disagreement on Value

One spouse may believe the home is worth significantly more than the other thinks. This disagreement can prevent listing, delay negotiations, and create resentment. Getting a professional appraisal (or two) early in the process can provide an objective anchor for discussions. In a cash sale, the buyer provides a firm offer based on their own assessment, which can help both parties move past the value debate.

One Spouse Still Living in the Home

It is common for one spouse to continue living in the home during divorce proceedings, especially if children are involved. This creates complications for showings, staging, and the overall presentation of the property. The occupying spouse may resist the disruption of a traditional sale, while the non-occupying spouse may push for a quick sale to access their equity. A cash sale that does not require showings or staging can resolve this tension.

Financial Pressure

Maintaining a mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and utilities on a home while also potentially funding a second household puts enormous financial strain on both parties. The longer the home takes to sell, the more money drains out. This financial pressure often makes the speed of a cash sale more valuable than the potentially higher price of a traditional listing.

Tax Implications of Selling During Divorce

The tax consequences of selling the marital home depend heavily on the timing of the sale relative to the divorce decree. Here are the key considerations:

  • Capital gains exclusion: If you file jointly in the year of the sale (which requires still being married), you may exclude up to $500,000 in capital gains from federal income tax. If the divorce is final before the sale and you file as single, each spouse can exclude up to $250,000 — but only if each meets the ownership and use requirements (owned and lived in the home for at least two of the last five years).
  • Timing matters: If one spouse moved out more than three years before the sale, that spouse may not meet the use requirement and could owe capital gains tax on their share of the proceeds. Planning the timing of the sale around these thresholds can save tens of thousands of dollars in taxes.
  • Transfer between spouses: Under IRC Section 1041, transfers of property between spouses (or former spouses if incident to divorce) are generally tax-free. This applies to buyout situations — the departing spouse does not owe tax on the transfer itself. However, the keeping spouse inherits the original cost basis, which could create a larger capital gains liability when they eventually sell.
  • California state taxes: California taxes capital gains as ordinary income, with rates up to 13.3%. The federal exclusion applies at the state level as well, making it especially important to qualify for the full exclusion.

Important: Tax law is complex and individual circumstances vary significantly. Always consult with a tax professional or CPA who has experience with divorce-related real estate transactions before making decisions based on tax considerations.

Court-Ordered Sales: When You Cannot Agree

When divorcing spouses simply cannot agree on what to do with the marital home — one wants to sell and the other refuses, or they cannot agree on terms — either party can ask the court to intervene. In California, there are two main paths:

Family Court Order

During divorce proceedings, a family court judge can order the sale of the home as part of dividing community property. The judge may appoint a real estate agent, set a listing price based on appraisals, and establish a process for the sale to proceed even if one spouse is uncooperative. While this resolves the impasse, it adds legal fees, court time, and delays.

Partition Action

A partition action is a separate legal proceeding in which one co-owner asks the court to divide or sell jointly owned property. In California, for residential real estate, the court will typically order a sale rather than a physical division of the property. The court may appoint a referee to manage the sale, and the proceeds are divided according to each party's ownership interest.

Partition actions are expensive — attorney fees, referee fees, and court costs can easily run $10,000-$30,000 or more. The process typically takes 6-12 months. For this reason, reaching an agreement through mediation or direct negotiation is almost always preferable, even if it means accepting a lower price through a cash sale rather than enduring the cost and delay of litigation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my house during a divorce without my spouse's consent in California?

Generally, no. California is a community property state, and the marital home is typically considered community property. Both spouses must agree to the sale. However, if you cannot reach an agreement, you can petition the court for a partition action, and a judge can order the sale of the property.

How is home equity divided in a California divorce?

Under California's community property laws, equity in the marital home is generally split 50/50 between both spouses. Equity is calculated by taking the home's current fair market value and subtracting the remaining mortgage balance and any selling costs. If one spouse made a separate property contribution (such as a down payment from an inheritance), that amount may be credited back before the equal split.

Do I have to pay capital gains tax when selling a house during divorce?

If the home was your primary residence and you lived in it for at least two of the last five years, you may qualify for the capital gains exclusion — up to $250,000 for a single filer or $500,000 if filing jointly. The timing of the sale relative to your divorce decree can affect which exclusion applies. It is important to consult with a tax professional to understand your specific situation.

How long does it take to sell a house during a divorce in California?

The timeline depends on the method of sale. Listing on the open market typically takes 3-6 months or longer, especially if both parties must agree on every decision. A cash sale to a company like Redwood Home Buyers can close in as few as 7-14 days, which is often preferred by divorcing couples who want a clean, fast resolution.

What happens to the house if we can't agree on what to do with it?

If you and your spouse cannot agree on whether to sell the home or how to handle it, either party can file a partition action with the court. A judge can order the property sold, with the proceeds divided according to community property law. This process adds time and legal costs, so reaching an agreement — even through mediation — is usually preferable.
