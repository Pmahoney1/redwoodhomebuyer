California Foreclosure Timeline: What Happens and When

By Patrick Mahoney June 13, 2026 8 min read

If you've missed a mortgage payment — or you're worried you might — understanding how foreclosure works in California is the first step toward protecting yourself. The process follows a specific legal timeline with built-in windows where you can take action. Knowing what happens at each stage, and when, gives you the power to make informed decisions before it's too late.

This guide walks you through every phase of the California foreclosure timeline, from your first missed payment all the way to the trustee sale. We'll also cover your legal rights, the options available to you at each stage, and how homeowners across Sacramento and Northern California are stopping foreclosure before it reaches the auction block.

California Is a Non-Judicial Foreclosure State — What That Means for You

California primarily uses non-judicial foreclosure, which means your lender does not need to go through the court system to foreclose on your home. Instead, the process is handled through a trustee — a neutral third party named in your deed of trust when you took out your mortgage.

Non-judicial foreclosure is faster and less expensive for lenders than going to court, which is why it's the method used in the vast majority of California foreclosures. For homeowners, the key implication is this: the timeline moves quickly, and there is no right of redemption after the sale. Once your home is sold at a trustee sale, the sale is final.

That said, California law includes significant protections for homeowners — particularly through the California Homeowner Bill of Rights — that give you time and leverage to pursue alternatives. Understanding the timeline is how you use those protections effectively.

The California Foreclosure Timeline: Step by Step

Here is exactly what happens, and when, from the moment you miss your first mortgage payment through the trustee sale.

Days 1-30: First Missed Payment and Grace Period

Most mortgage agreements include a grace period of 10 to 15 days after your payment due date. If your payment is due on the 1st of the month, you typically have until the 15th or 16th before your lender considers the payment late and charges a late fee.

At this stage, nothing is reported to credit bureaus, and no formal foreclosure process has begun. However, once you pass the grace period and your payment is officially 30 days late, your lender will report the delinquency to the credit bureaus. A single 30-day late payment can drop your credit score by 60 to 110 points.

What you should do: If you know you're going to miss a payment, call your lender before the due date. Lenders have loss mitigation departments specifically designed to help borrowers who are struggling. Reaching out early shows good faith and opens the door to options like forbearance or a repayment plan before things escalate.

Days 30-120: Lender Contact, Loss Mitigation, and Breach Letter

Under federal law (the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act), your mortgage servicer must attempt to contact you within 36 days of a missed payment to discuss loss mitigation options. By 45 days, they must send you a written notice describing available assistance programs.

During this window, you may receive a breach letter (also called a demand letter or notice of intent to accelerate). This letter formally notifies you that you're in default and typically gives you 30 days to cure the default — meaning to pay all past-due amounts — before the lender takes the next step.

Critically, federal law prohibits lenders from filing foreclosure until the borrower is at least 120 days delinquent. This 120-day rule is one of the most important protections you have. It gives you roughly four months from your first missed payment to explore alternatives before any formal foreclosure paperwork is filed.

What you should do: Use this time wisely. Apply for a loan modification, explore forbearance options, or consult with a HUD-approved housing counselor. If your financial hardship is temporary — a job loss, medical issue, or unexpected expense — there's a good chance your lender will work with you. If the situation is more permanent, this is the time to evaluate whether selling your home quickly might be the smartest move to protect your equity and credit.

Day 120+: Notice of Default (NOD) Filed

Once you are at least 120 days behind, your lender can instruct the trustee to file a Notice of Default (NOD) with your county recorder's office. This is the official start of the California foreclosure process.

The NOD is a public record. It will include the amount you owe to bring the loan current, and it is sent to you by certified mail and posted on the property. The filing of the NOD triggers a 90-day reinstatement period — a window during which you have the legal right to stop the foreclosure by paying all past-due amounts, plus any fees and costs incurred by the lender.

During these 90 days, the trustee cannot move forward with scheduling a sale. This reinstatement period is one of your most powerful protections, and it's enshrined in California Civil Code Section 2924c.

What you should do: This is a critical decision point. If you can come up with the past-due amount (through savings, borrowing from family, or other means), you can reinstate the loan and stop the process entirely. If reinstatement isn't feasible, you still have time to pursue a loan modification, negotiate a short sale, or sell the home to a cash buyer like Redwood Home Buyers. Don't wait until the 90 days are up — every day you delay narrows your options.

90 Days After NOD: Notice of Trustee Sale (NOTS)

If you haven't cured the default during the 90-day reinstatement period, the trustee can record a Notice of Trustee Sale (NOTS) with the county. This notice sets the date, time, and location of the foreclosure auction.

Under California law, the trustee sale cannot take place until at least 21 days after the NOTS is recorded. The notice must also be:

  • Mailed to you at least 20 days before the sale
  • Posted on the property at least 20 days before the sale
  • Published in a local newspaper once a week for three consecutive weeks

Even after the NOTS is recorded, you still have options. Under California law, you can reinstate the loan up to five business days before the scheduled trustee sale by paying all past-due amounts, fees, and costs. This is your last opportunity to stop the foreclosure by catching up on payments.

What you should do: If the auction is scheduled, the clock is ticking — but you're not out of options yet. A cash home sale can often close faster than the 21+ days between the NOTS and the auction. Contact us for a cash offer and we can work with the trustee to ensure the sale closes and the foreclosure is canceled before auction day.

The Trustee Sale (Auction)

On the scheduled date, the property is sold at public auction — typically on the steps of the county courthouse or at another designated public location. The opening bid is usually the amount owed on the mortgage plus fees and costs, though the lender can set a lower opening bid.

If a third-party bidder offers more than the opening bid, they purchase the property and must pay in full (usually cashier's check) immediately. If no one bids, the property reverts to the lender and becomes REO (Real Estate Owned) — essentially, the bank takes ownership.

The trustee sale is the point of no return. Once the sale is completed and the trustee's deed is recorded, the former homeowner has no legal right to reclaim the property. California does not provide a statutory right of redemption after a non-judicial foreclosure sale.

Post-Sale: What Happens Next

After the trustee sale, if you're still living in the property, the new owner (either the third-party buyer or the bank) must provide you with a written notice to vacate. In most cases, former homeowners are given a brief period to move out — typically 3 days under an unlawful detainer action, though some buyers negotiate longer move-out periods.

On the financial side, a foreclosure stays on your credit report for seven years and can drop your credit score by 100 to 160 points or more. You'll also face a waiting period before you can qualify for a new conventional mortgage — typically seven years for a conventional loan, three years for an FHA loan.

One important protection: under California Code of Civil Procedure Section 580d, lenders generally cannot pursue a deficiency judgment after a non-judicial foreclosure on a purchase-money mortgage. This means if your home sells at auction for less than you owed, the lender typically cannot come after you for the difference. However, if you refinanced or have second liens, different rules may apply — consult with an attorney.

Your Rights Under the California Homeowner Bill of Rights

California's Homeowner Bill of Rights (HBOR), enacted in 2012 and updated since, provides some of the strongest foreclosure protections in the nation. Here's what you need to know:

  • Ban on dual tracking: Your lender cannot advance the foreclosure process while simultaneously reviewing your application for a loan modification or other loss mitigation option. If you've submitted a complete application, the foreclosure must pause until a decision is made.
  • Single point of contact: Your mortgage servicer must assign you a single point of contact — a specific person or team — who can answer your questions about loss mitigation options and ensure your application is properly processed.
  • Written denial with reasons: If your loan modification application is denied, the servicer must provide a written explanation of the reasons and inform you of any appeal rights.
  • Restrictions on fees: Servicers cannot charge borrowers fees for certain documents related to the foreclosure process, including copies of the note and deed of trust.
  • Verification of documents: The person recording the Notice of Default and the person conducting the trustee sale must verify the accuracy of the foreclosure documents under penalty of perjury.

These protections are powerful, but you need to assert them. If your lender violates the HBOR — for example, by moving forward with foreclosure while your loan modification application is pending — you may have grounds to delay or halt the process. Contact a housing attorney or HUD-approved housing counselor if you believe your rights are being violated.

Options to Stop Foreclosure at Every Stage

No matter where you are in the timeline, you likely have options. Here's what's available at each phase:

Reinstatement

Pay all past-due payments, plus fees and costs, to bring the loan current. Available from the first missed payment all the way up to five business days before the trustee sale. This is often the simplest option if you've recovered from a temporary hardship and have access to the funds.

Loan Modification

Your lender may agree to change the terms of your loan — lowering the interest rate, extending the term, or reducing the principal — to make payments more affordable. Best pursued early in the process, before the Notice of Default is filed. Under the HBOR dual tracking ban, applying for a modification can also pause the foreclosure timeline.

Forbearance

A forbearance agreement temporarily reduces or suspends your mortgage payments for a set period, giving you time to recover from a financial hardship. At the end of the forbearance period, you'll need to repay the missed amounts — either in a lump sum, through a repayment plan, or via a loan modification.

Sell the Home

If you have equity in your home, selling it before the foreclosure is completed allows you to pay off the mortgage, protect your credit, and potentially walk away with cash. A traditional sale through a real estate agent can maximize your price, but it typically takes 60 to 90 days — time you may not have. Selling to a cash home buyer is significantly faster, with closings possible in as few as 7 days.

Short Sale

If you owe more than your home is worth (you're "underwater"), your lender may agree to accept less than the full balance through a short sale. This requires lender approval and typically takes 2 to 4 months, but it's far less damaging to your credit than a foreclosure.

Bankruptcy

Filing for Chapter 13 bankruptcy triggers an automatic stay that immediately halts the foreclosure process. Chapter 13 allows you to reorganize your debts and catch up on missed mortgage payments over a 3-to-5-year repayment plan. This is a serious step with long-term financial consequences and should only be pursued after consulting with a bankruptcy attorney.

Sacramento-Specific Resources for Homeowners Facing Foreclosure

If you're a homeowner in the Sacramento area, these resources can help:

  • California Mortgage Relief Program: This state-funded program provides up to $80,000 in assistance for past-due mortgage payments, property taxes, and other housing costs. Funded through the federal American Rescue Plan Act, the program is available to eligible California homeowners who experienced a COVID-related financial hardship. Apply at CaMortgageRelief.org.
  • Sacramento County Department of Human Assistance: Offers emergency housing assistance programs and can connect you with local resources for financial counseling and support.
  • HUD-Approved Housing Counseling Agencies: Free, confidential counseling is available through HUD-approved agencies in the Sacramento area. Counselors can help you understand your options, communicate with your lender, and navigate the loss mitigation process. Find a counselor near you at hud.gov/counseling.
  • Legal Aid: Organizations like Legal Services of Northern California offer free legal assistance to low-income homeowners facing foreclosure. They can review your case, ensure your lender is following the law, and represent you if your rights have been violated.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does the foreclosure process take in California?

The California non-judicial foreclosure process typically takes approximately 200 days (roughly 6-7 months) from the first missed payment to the trustee sale. However, timelines can vary. Lenders must wait at least 120 days before filing a Notice of Default, then there is a 90-day reinstatement period, followed by at least 21 days before the auction. Delays from loss mitigation reviews, loan modification applications, or legal challenges can extend the timeline significantly.

Can I stop a foreclosure once a Notice of Default has been filed?

Yes. You have a 90-day reinstatement period after the Notice of Default is recorded during which you can cure the default by paying all past-due amounts plus fees and costs. You can also stop foreclosure by obtaining a loan modification, selling the home (including to a cash buyer), filing for bankruptcy, or negotiating a short sale with your lender. Even after a Notice of Trustee Sale is recorded, you can reinstate the loan up to five business days before the auction date.

What is the difference between a Notice of Default and a Notice of Trustee Sale?

A Notice of Default (NOD) is the formal document recorded with the county that starts the foreclosure clock. It triggers a 90-day reinstatement period during which you can catch up on missed payments. A Notice of Trustee Sale (NOTS) is filed after the reinstatement period expires and sets the actual auction date, which must be at least 21 days after the NOTS is recorded. The NOTS is the final warning before your home is sold at auction.

Is there a right of redemption after foreclosure in California?

No. In California non-judicial foreclosures (which account for the vast majority of foreclosures in the state), there is no statutory right of redemption after the trustee sale. Once the property is sold at auction, the sale is final. This makes it critical to act before the auction date. In the rare case of a judicial foreclosure, there may be a redemption period, but this process is seldom used in California.

Will I owe money after my home is foreclosed in California?

Under California law (Code of Civil Procedure Section 580d), lenders generally cannot pursue a deficiency judgment after a non-judicial foreclosure on a purchase-money loan. This means if your original mortgage was used to buy your home and the home sells at auction for less than what you owe, the lender typically cannot come after you for the difference. However, if you refinanced or took out a second mortgage, different rules may apply. Consult with an attorney to understand your specific situation.

Can selling my house to a cash buyer stop foreclosure?

Yes. Selling to a cash home buyer is one of the fastest ways to stop foreclosure. Cash buyers like Redwood Home Buyers can close in as few as 7 days — well before most trustee sale dates. The sale proceeds pay off your outstanding mortgage balance, the foreclosure is canceled, and your credit is protected from the severe damage of a completed foreclosure. You may also walk away with remaining equity in your pocket.
