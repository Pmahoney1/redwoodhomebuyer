Selling a Sonoma County House With Title Problems: Liens, Clouded Title and Missing Heirs

By Patrick Mahoney October 25, 2026 9 min read

Most houses in Sonoma County sell without anyone thinking about title. The preliminary report comes back clean, the lender funds, escrow records the deed, and the seller is paid. The houses that do not sell that way are the ones we hear about: a Permit Sonoma abatement lien discovered on day twenty of escrow, a parent's name still on the deed years after their death, a loan paid off in 2009 that still shows as an open deed of trust, a fire lot in Fountaingrove with an insurance claim and a half-released construction loan tangled together.

If that sounds like your property, this guide explains what the problem actually is, why it stops a traditional sale, and how a cash buyer that does its own curative title work can close anyway. It covers the problems we see most often in Sonoma County, from Santa Rosa tract homes to West County parcels on wells and septic. It is general information, not legal advice; where a dispute is involved, a real estate attorney belongs in the conversation.

What a "title problem" actually is

Title is the record of who owns a property and who else has a claim against it. When you open escrow, the title company searches the Sonoma County Clerk-Recorder's records and issues a preliminary title report. Anything that could challenge the buyer's ownership or sit ahead of the buyer's lender shows up as an exception: liens, judgments, recorded notices, unreleased deeds of trust, gaps in the chain of ownership, easements nobody mentioned.

A title company will insure around small, routine exceptions. It will not insure around an unresolved one. Once the title company declines coverage, the buyer's lender declines to fund, because no lender will lend against a property it cannot insure. At that point every buyer who needs a mortgage is gone, and in Sonoma County that is most of the market. The property has not lost value. It has lost its buyers.

The title problems we see most in Sonoma County

Permit Sonoma code violations and abatement liens

In the unincorporated county, code enforcement runs through Permit Sonoma at 2550 Ventura Avenue. Unpermitted construction, a garage conversion, grading, a failed septic repair, junk vehicles or a cannabis grow all produce a case. Under County Code section 1-7.1, residential penalties run from $15 to $100 a day, or a multiple of the permit fee, with each day a separate offense. Once the county records a Notice of Abatement Proceedings, its own guidance says the notice can prevent a sale, refinancing or title insurance. Partial and supplemental abatement liens follow if the violation is not corrected.

Owners have thirty days to abate and ten days to appeal. Many cannot do either, because the cure is a permit they cannot get or a demolition they cannot afford. The county does offer an innocent-purchaser exclusion: penalties can be waived when a violation predates the purchase, the buyer had no notice, and abatement begins within thirty days. That exclusion is one reason a buyer like us can take on a violation property that a financed buyer cannot touch. For city-limits properties in Santa Rosa, Petaluma and the other eight cities, the city's own code enforcement applies, and the same logic holds. Our guide to selling a house with code violations covers the disclosure side.

Property taxes in default

After five years of unpaid property taxes, the Auditor-Controller-Treasurer-Tax Collector can sell the parcel at a tax-defaulted land auction. In the November 2025 Sonoma County sale, 57 parcels were listed, 34 were redeemed by their owners before the auction, and 18 sold. Owners can redeem until five o'clock the business day before the sale. Those numbers tell the real story: most people cure at the last minute, often by selling. Back taxes, penalties and redemption fees are paid from the sale proceeds at closing, so a tax default is usually one of the easier problems to solve, as long as the sale closes before the auction date.

An inherited house that never went through probate

This is the most common serious problem we see. A parent passes away, a child moves in or keeps paying the taxes, and the deed stays in the parent's name for years. When the family finally decides to sell, no one has legal authority to sign the deed. If the home was in a living trust, the successor trustee can sell. If it was not, the estate goes through probate at the Sonoma County Superior Court. Probate moved in July 2026 to the new Hall of Justice at 625 Administration Drive in Santa Rosa, Department 63 on the sixth floor. Many websites, including some competitors, still list the old Cleveland Avenue courtroom.

Two other things change the math for heirs. Under Proposition 19, an inherited home is reassessed to market value unless the child moves in as a primary residence within a year, and even then the exclusion is capped at just over one million dollars of value through February 2027. A Rohnert Park ranch house assessed at $180,000 on a 1978 basis can carry a tax bill several times larger the year after a parent's death. And when several heirs share the inheritance, one holdout can stall everything. Our article on selling an inherited house in California walks through the stepped-up basis and the timeline. We can open escrow while a probate petition is pending and close when the court grants authority.

A paid-off loan that was never reconveyed

When a mortgage is paid off, the lender is supposed to record a reconveyance that releases the deed of trust. Lenders miss this constantly, especially ones that were acquired or failed in 2008 and 2009. The loan is gone but the lien is still on record, and the title company treats it as an open debt. Clearing it means obtaining a reconveyance from the successor lender, or, when the lender cannot be found, having the title company accept a bond or a sworn statement under California's reconveyance statutes. It is tedious, not hard, and it is the kind of work we do on our own files rather than asking the seller to chase it.

Mechanics liens, judgments and tax liens

A contractor who was not paid for a Coffey Park rebuild can record a mechanics lien. A creditor with a court judgment can record an abstract of judgment. The IRS, the Franchise Tax Board, the county, a former spouse owed support, and a homeowners association can all record liens. Each one has its own release process and its own negotiating room. Some liens are stale and can be challenged. Some lienholders accept less than face value for a quick payoff. A seller facing four liens from four directions usually cannot sort that out while a financed buyer waits. A buyer that does this routinely can.

Unpermitted ADUs, garage conversions and additions

Sonoma County is full of granny units, converted garages and added rooms that were never permitted, particularly in West County and the Sonoma Valley. They rarely show up as a recorded lien, but they surface in the appraisal, the inspection or a permit search, and a lender will not count unpermitted square footage. County Ordinance 6544, in effect since January 8, 2026, allows accessory units built before 2020 to be permitted after the fact if they are not substandard. Sometimes the right answer is to legalize and then list. Sometimes the owner has neither the time nor the money, and selling as-is to a buyer who will do the permitting is the better outcome.

Fire lots and stalled rebuilds

Nine years after the Tubbs Fire, Fountaingrove, Larkfield-Wikiup and Mark West still hold vacant lots and unfinished rebuilds. These parcels carry problems most buyers have never seen: an insurance payout with the lender named as payee, a construction loan partially drawn and partially released, a deed of trust that was supposed to be reconveyed when the claim settled, and in some cases a lot that changed hands informally between family members. The Kincade and Glass fire zones have smaller versions of the same story. We buy fire lots and partial rebuilds and resolve the lien stack as part of the purchase.

Boundaries, easements and access on rural parcels

On the river, the coast and in the hills west of Sebastopol, the recorded description and the fence line do not always agree. Shared wells, driveways that cross a neighbor's land without a recorded easement, and road-maintenance agreements that exist only as handshakes are common. A title company will except them. A survey and a recorded easement can fix them, but that takes a cooperative neighbor and several weeks. Where the neighbor is not cooperative, the sale needs a buyer who will take the parcel with the exception and resolve it afterward.

Why the traditional sale stalls

The pattern is nearly always the same. The house is listed and goes into contract. The preliminary title report arrives in the first week of escrow and shows the exception. The listing agent asks the seller to clear it. The seller calls the county, the old lender or a sibling, and gets nowhere in the time available. Around day thirty the buyer's lender confirms it will not fund. The buyer cancels, the house goes back on the market with a longer days-on-market count, and the next buyer's escrow finds the same exception. Sellers routinely lose two or three months this way, and in a fire-zone neighborhood where insurance is already shrinking the buyer pool, the second listing can be harder than the first.

How a curative-title cash buyer closes anyway

We do not need a lender, so we do not need the title company to insure a clean title on the day we sign. That single fact changes what is possible. In practice a purchase works like this:

  • We review the preliminary title report ourselves and tell you which exceptions are routine payoffs, which need a release or reconveyance, and which require a court process.
  • Liens are paid from proceeds at closing. Taxes, abatement liens, judgments and association dues come off the top of the price. You never write a check.
  • We negotiate releases. Where a lienholder will accept less, or a lien is stale, we handle that conversation and the recording.
  • We take properties subject to the problem when that is the only way to close on your timeline, and resolve it after closing at our cost. Permit Sonoma's innocent-purchaser rule and after-the-fact ADU permitting make this workable for violation properties.
  • Probate and quiet title happen on our side. If the estate needs a petition or an ownership dispute needs a court order, we can fund the attorney's work and close when the order is granted, or close in two stages with part of the price paid at the start.
  • You get one net number. The offer already reflects the cure, so there are no later deductions, no commissions, no repair credits and no re-trades when the title report arrives.

The timeline depends on the problem. A payoff adds nothing to a seven to fourteen day close. A missing reconveyance adds two to six weeks. A probate petition adds months, though you know the price from the start. Our process page covers the mechanics of the offer itself.

What to gather before you call anyone

  • The most recent deed and any prior preliminary title report, if an earlier escrow fell through.
  • Every notice from Permit Sonoma or a city code-enforcement office, with dates.
  • The property tax bill and any default or redemption statement from the tax collector.
  • Payoff letters or statements for any loan, including ones you believe are paid off.
  • Death certificates, the will or trust, and any letters issued by the probate court.
  • Lien notices, judgments, HOA statements and insurance claim correspondence for fire properties.

You do not need all of it to get an offer. A property address and a plain description of the problem is enough for us to pull the records and tell you what we see.

How the offer is priced

A cash offer on a clean house is below the price a perfectly staged listing might fetch after sixty days and a six percent commission. A cash offer on a house with a title problem is priced against a different alternative: a listing that cannot close at all. The offer reflects the market value of the property, less the cost and time of curing the specific defects and the risk that a cure takes longer than planned. We show that math rather than hiding it. For many sellers the comparison that matters is not our price against a hypothetical retail sale, but our price against the auction date, the daily penalty, or another year of a reassessed tax bill on a house they do not live in.

When you need an attorney first

Some situations should start with a lawyer rather than a buyer: a deed you believe was forged, heirs who dispute the will, a divorce where the property division is unsettled, or a lender threatening a sale date within days. In those cases we are glad to work alongside your attorney, and a sale is often still the resolution, but the legal question has to be framed first. For everything else on this list, a conversation with a buyer who does this work is the faster first step.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell a house in Sonoma County with a lien on it?

Yes. Most liens are paid from the sale proceeds at closing, and the escrow officer records the releases. The sale only stalls when the liens exceed the equity, when a lienholder cannot be found, or when a buyer's lender refuses to fund until a cloud is cleared. A cash buyer who handles curative title can close in those situations.

What is a Notice of Abatement from Permit Sonoma and does it stop a sale?

It is the county's recorded notice that an unresolved code violation exists on the parcel. Permit Sonoma states that the notice can prevent a sale, refinancing or title insurance, and that abatement liens can follow. You can still sell to a buyer willing to take the property with the violation and correct it after closing, which is how we buy.

My parent died and the house is still in their name. Can I sell it?

Not until the estate has authority to transfer title. If the home was in a living trust, the successor trustee can sell. If not, the estate usually goes through probate at the Sonoma County Superior Court, which handles probate in Department 63 at 625 Administration Drive in Santa Rosa. We can start the purchase while the petition is pending and close once the court grants authority.

How long does it take to clear a title problem before closing?

A straightforward lien payoff adds no time. A missing reconveyance from a paid-off loan usually takes two to six weeks. A probate petition takes several months, and a quiet title action to resolve an ownership dispute can take six months or more. We tell you which category your file falls into after reviewing the preliminary title report.

Who pays to fix the title problem when I sell to a cash buyer?

We do. Our offer already reflects the cost and time of the cure, so you do not hire an attorney or front any money. You receive a net figure up front and the title work happens on our side of the transaction.
