How to Sell a Rental Property in California When You Are Done Being a Landlord

By Patrick Mahoney October 26, 2026 11 min read

You own a rental and you are done with it. Maybe the tenant stopped paying. Maybe the last vacancy cost you two months of rent and a new water heater. Maybe you inherited your parents' duplex, you live three hours away, and every phone call is a problem. Or maybe nothing dramatic happened. You are just tired of being a landlord, and the rules in California keep getting harder to follow.

Selling a rental is not the same as selling the house you live in. There is a tenant with rights, a lease that outlives you as the owner, and a tax bill at the end. This post walks through what the situation actually means for a sale, your real options, how a cash sale with tenants in place works, and what to have ready before you call. If you are weighing this against other hard situations, our situations we help page covers the rest.

What owning a rental means for the sale

The lease goes with the house. When you sell, the buyer steps into your shoes as landlord. The tenant keeps the same lease, the same rent, and the same rights. A sale does not end a tenancy, and in California it is generally not, by itself, a legal reason to make a tenant leave. Any buyer who tells you otherwise is guessing.

Tenant protections, in general terms

California has a statewide law, usually called AB 1482, that caps annual rent increases and requires a just cause to end most tenancies once a tenant has been in place long enough. It has exemptions, and some single-family homes fall under them, but the rules for claiming an exemption are specific and easy to get wrong. On top of the state law, a number of cities and counties have their own ordinances that add stricter caps, broader just-cause rules, relocation payments, or registration requirements. Owner move-in and sale are not automatic grounds to end a tenancy. Each has conditions, notice periods, and in some places relocation money attached. Before you send a tenant any notice, talk to a landlord-tenant attorney in your county. We are not attorneys.

Why landlords reach this point

  • Repairs. Roofs, sewer lines, and water heaters do not care that the rent is late.
  • Vacancies and turnover. Cleaning, painting, screening, and weeks without rent.
  • Problem tenants. Nonpayment, damage, unauthorized occupants, or complaints to the city.
  • Rules that keep changing. Rent caps, just-cause rules, registries, and inspections that differ by city.
  • Retirement. You want the equity in a simpler form and your weekends back.
  • Inherited rentals. You never chose to be a landlord. If this is you, our guide on selling an inherited house in California covers the probate and tax side.
  • Distance. You moved, the rental did not, and the manager costs more than the problems they solve.

What a financed buyer wants versus what a cash buyer accepts

A buyer using a mortgage usually wants the property vacant at closing, clean, and inspected, with an appraisal that supports the price. A below-market rent on a long lease is a problem for the lender and the buyer. Deferred maintenance can stall the loan at inspection or appraisal. A cash buyer does not have those limits. We buy occupied rentals, honor the existing lease, and take the property with whatever maintenance has been put off. The rent, lease terms, and deposit are disclosed through escrow and transfer to the new owner at closing.

What a listing with tenants in place looks like

Agents will tell you a tenant-occupied listing is fine. Sometimes it is. Often it goes like this. The tenant limits or refuses showings, which they can generally do without proper notice. Buyers who get in see someone else's clutter and walk. The ones who stay ask for concessions because they cannot move in, renovate, or raise the rent. Your agent suggests a price cut. If you pay the tenant to leave so you can list vacant, you have added cost and delay, and in some cities that agreement has its own rules. Meanwhile the mortgage, taxes, and insurance keep coming due.

Your options, honestly

  • List with an agent. This is the better route if the property is vacant or will be soon, it is in good shape, and you can wait through the marketing, inspections, appraisal, and a possible loan falling through. You will pay commissions and likely some repairs or credits, but a clean vacant rental in a strong neighborhood should bring a higher price this way.
  • Sell to a cash buyer. This is the better route if the tenant is staying, the rent is below market, the property needs work, or you simply want it done on a date you pick. You trade some price for certainty, speed, and no repairs or fees. Our post on cash buyers versus agents in California lays out that tradeoff in detail.
  • Wait for the lease to end. If the lease expires in a few months and the tenant plans to move anyway, waiting can make sense. It does not make sense if you would be relying on a notice to end the tenancy, because in many places that is not a simple or quick process.
  • Keep it and hire a manager. A good manager fixes some problems. It does not fix a bad roof, a below-market lease, or your wish to be out.

What to have ready before you call

  • The current lease or rental agreement, including any addenda or month-to-month history.
  • Current rent, date of the last increase, and the deposit you hold.
  • Whether the tenant is current and whether any notices have been served.
  • Your most recent mortgage statement, if there is a loan.
  • Property tax status and any HOA information.
  • Any code enforcement letters, rental registration, or inspection records. If there are open violations, our guide to selling a house with code violations in California explains how those are handled.
  • If you inherited the rental, the deed or trust showing how title is held.
  • A list of known problems with the property. We will see them anyway, and knowing up front speeds up the offer.

How this works across Northern California

The statewide rules are the same everywhere, but the local layer is not. Some cities add their own rent caps, just-cause ordinances, registries, and relocation requirements. Others follow state law only. Verify the rules for your specific city before you act.

We buy occupied and vacant rentals in all ten counties below. Each line notes something specific to that county, and each links to our county page.

  • Sonoma County: the unincorporated county has its own tenant protections ordinance on top of state law, and Santa Rosa and Petaluma have local rental rules of their own, so check with your city before giving notice.
  • Sacramento County: the City of Sacramento runs a Tenant Protection Program with its own rent cap, registration, and just-cause rules for covered units, while the unincorporated county follows state law.
  • Contra Costa County: Richmond, Concord, and Antioch each have their own rent ordinances with different coverage, caps, and registries, the most varied patchwork of any county we serve.
  • Napa County: we have not found a local rent or just-cause ordinance beyond state law in the City of Napa, so confirm with the city; we buy in Napa, American Canyon, St. Helena, and Calistoga.
  • Mendocino County: state law applies without a local ordinance that we know of, and Ukiah has a large share of renters, so occupied duplexes are common; we buy in Ukiah, Fort Bragg, and Willits.
  • Marin County: Fairfax, San Anselmo, and Larkspur have each adopted local just-cause or rent rules in recent years, some challenged at the ballot, so check current status.
  • Solano County: no local rent control or just-cause ordinance in Vallejo or the county as of our last check, so state law governs; we buy in Vallejo, Fairfield, Vacaville, Suisun City, Benicia, and Dixon.
  • Placer County: no local ordinance that we have found, so state law applies; we buy in Roseville, Rocklin, Lincoln, Auburn, and Loomis.
  • El Dorado County: state law applies with no local rent ordinance we have found, and South Lake Tahoe's short-term rental rules have changed more than once, which matters if yours was a vacation unit.
  • Nevada County: no county-level rent stabilization that we have found, so state law applies; we buy in Grass Valley, Nevada City, Penn Valley, and Truckee.

When you need a professional first

Call a landlord-tenant attorney before you serve any notice, pay a tenant to leave, or claim an exemption from the state rent cap. The rules differ by city and the penalties for getting them wrong fall on you, not the buyer. Call a CPA before you accept any offer. Selling a rental usually means capital gains tax on the increase in value since you bought it, plus recapture of the depreciation you took, and the closing date can change which tax year it lands in. If you are thinking about rolling the proceeds into another property through a 1031 exchange, ask your CPA before escrow opens, because the deadlines start at closing. Once you know where you stand, call us. You get a written number within 24 hours and the decision is yours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my rental in California while the tenant is still living there?

Yes. The property sells with the lease attached, and the buyer becomes the new landlord under the same terms. You do not have to wait for a vacancy. Financed buyers often want the unit empty, but a cash buyer can take it occupied. Any notice to your tenant should be reviewed by a landlord-tenant attorney first, because sale alone is generally not grounds to end a tenancy.

Do I have to tell my tenant I am selling the house?

There is generally no rule that you must announce a sale in advance, but your tenant will need proper notice before anyone enters the unit, and they will need to know where to send rent after closing. Most sellers tell the tenant once an offer is accepted. The lease, the deposit, and the rent terms carry over to the new owner, which is usually the tenant's main concern.

What happens to the security deposit when I sell a rental property?

In general, the deposit stays with the property. It is accounted for in escrow and credited to the buyer, who then holds it for the tenant under the same terms. You should have an accurate deposit figure and any deductions documented before closing. If you are unsure how your deposit was handled over the years, sort that out with your attorney or CPA before you accept an offer.

Will I owe taxes when I sell a rental I have owned for years?

Usually, yes. Selling a rental typically triggers capital gains tax on the increase in value and recapture of the depreciation you claimed while renting it out. The exact amount depends on your basis, how long you owned it, and your overall income. A 1031 exchange may defer some of it if you buy another investment property, but the deadlines are strict. Ask your CPA before you sign anything.

Is it better to wait until the tenant leaves before I sell?

It depends on the lease and your patience. If the lease ends soon and the tenant plans to move, waiting can make a listing easier and may bring a higher price. If the tenant is month to month or long term, waiting means relying on a notice process that is slow and regulated in many California cities. A cash buyer removes that problem by taking the property occupied.
